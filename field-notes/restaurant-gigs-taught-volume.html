What Restaurant Gigs Have Taught Me About Volume, Song Choice, and Reading the Room

After dozens of restaurant and winery performances, here's what I've learned about matching music to the room, managing volume, and knowing when to play versus when to sit back.

By Devin Frank

Working musician, restaurant performer, venue experimenter

The Hardest Venue to Play: The Quiet Restaurant

You'd think the hardest place to play would be a loud wedding reception or a festival stage. But honestly? It's a quiet upscale restaurant on a Tuesday night.

Here's why: At a festival, you can turn up. At a loud wedding reception, you've got inherent energy working with you. But at a quiet restaurant—especially a nice one where people come to have conversations—you need to be present without being loud, musical without being intrusive, and professional without disappearing.

I've learned more about performance, presence, and adaptation from restaurant gigs than anywhere else.

Lesson 1: Volume Isn't Just About Loudness

When I first started doing restaurant gigs, I thought "background music" meant turning my keyboard down and playing softly. That was wrong.

The volume of a performance—especially solo—isn't just about the decibel level. It's about:

  • Dynamic range: How much you vary between soft and loud passages. In a restaurant, you want your loud parts to be audible but not startling, and your soft parts to be present, not inaudible.
  • Frequency balance: A keyboard that's too heavy on bass can overpower a dining room even at moderate volumes. High frequencies cut through without intensity.
  • Space and air: A sparse, open arrangement can feel present at low volume. Thick, dense playing can feel loud and intrusive even at medium volume.
  • Clarity: If your playing is clear and musical, you don't need to be loud to be heard and appreciated.

The biggest breakthrough was realizing that playing "background music" doesn't mean playing quietly. It means playing present but not demanding.

Lesson 2: Song Choice Changes Throughout the Night

A restaurant fills and empties. The energy shifts. Early dinner is different from late dinner. A Friday night is different from a Tuesday night.

The song list that works for 6 PM might not work at 8 PM. Here's what I've learned:

Early Dining (5 PM – 7 PM)

The room is filling up. People are settling in. They want something familiar and relaxing. More standards, softer songs, nothing too adventurous. Familiar covers of classic piano songs work well.

Mid Dining (7 PM – 9 PM)

The room is full. Conversation is flowing. You can introduce more variety. A few new songs, some jazz, some of the songs people actually request. The energy is there to support a broader palette.

Late Dining (9 PM +)

The room is thinner, but the energy is different. Lingering guests might be more engaged. You might pick up energy from a bar if the restaurant has one. This is when I might play something a bit more contemporary or take more risks with arrangement.

Lesson 3: Reading the Room Means Watching, Not Listening to Yourself

This is the hardest lesson to internalize. As a performer, your instinct is to focus on your own playing: Did I hit that note? Was the tempo good? Does this arrangement work?

But in a restaurant, your job is to serve the room's experience, not your own.

Reading the room means:

  • Watch the dining groups. Are people leaning in and talking, or are they quiet? Are they checking their phones or engaged? Are they glancing over at you or ignoring you? None of these are bad—they tell you if your volume and energy are right.
  • Notice when people look up. If people consistently look at you when you start a new song or passage, your dynamics or song choice might be too strong for that moment. If no one even glances up, you might be too quiet or too background.
  • Listen to the room's feedback, not the sound of your own instrument. Your keyboard sounds great to you from the bench. But from across the dining room? That's the test. You often can't hear yourself accurately while you're playing—you're biased by being the source.
  • Adjust between songs, not during them. Watch how people respond to a full song, then adjust for the next one. You're gathering information and adapting.

Lesson 4: Sometimes the Best Performance is Knowing When to Stop

Early in my restaurant gig experience, I thought playing the full duration meant playing the entire time. If I was booked for 3 hours, I should be playing for 3 hours.

Wrong.

The best restaurants understand that live music is most impactful when it's a thoughtful presence, not a constant presence. Some of my most successful 3-hour gigs included 20-30 minute breaks where I stopped, sat down, and let the room breathe.

That break does multiple things:

  • It gives you a chance to reset and watch the room without playing
  • It reminds people that there's a person there—you're not a jukebox
  • When you come back, the music feels fresh, not like background noise
  • It prevents fatigue, for you and the audience

The venues that explicitly told me, "Play when it feels right, take breaks when you need them," had some of my best performances.

Lesson 5: Your Song List Matters More Than Your Virtuosity

I can play a stunning fingerstyle version of a classical piece that would impress any music student. But in a restaurant? It might clear the room or, at best, be ignored.

The songs that consistently draw positive response in restaurants are:

  • Familiar songs people recognize: They don't have to hear the whole thing to appreciate it. "Oh, I love that song" is a complete thought.
  • Songs with strong melody: Even at low volumes, a beautiful, clear melody cuts through and is memorable.
  • Songs that match the venue's aesthetic: A romantic restaurant wants romantic songs. A casual wine bar wants something lighter.
  • A mix of eras: Not all standards, not all contemporary. A spread that appeals to the mix of ages usually in a dining room.

I've gotten more appreciation from a tasteful arrangement of "Autumn Leaves" than from my best original composition. That's just the reality of restaurant gigs. Your job is to enhance the room, not showcase yourself.

Lesson 6: Communication With the Venue is Essential

Before your first gig at a restaurant, ask:

  • What time should I start and stop?
  • What's the typical guest count and pace?
  • Is there a particular vibe they want—sophisticated, casual, energetic, relaxed?
  • Are there any songs they love or hate?
  • What's the backup plan if my keyboard fails?
  • Is there a way to cue me when they want me to wrap up?

I once played a gig where I played upbeat jazz standards the whole night, thinking the energy was perfect. The manager later told me the venue wanted more classical, romantic material. We could have avoided a disconnect with a simple pre-show conversation.

Lesson 7: Your Gear Matters, But Your Adaptability Matters More

I've played restaurants where the audio system was perfect. I've played restaurants where my keyboard was routed through a poor PA system that compressed all the high-end out of my sound.

The best performances weren't always in ideal sound conditions. They were where I adapted: adjusted my EQ, simplified my texture, or chose songs that sounded better in the available equipment.

A performer who can't adapt to the room's constraints is dead weight, no matter how good their gear is.

Takeaways for Your Next Restaurant Gig

If you're about to book restaurant performances:

  • Prepare a setlist, but know that it will change. Have songs ready, but be willing to pivot based on how the room is feeling.
  • Set your volume deliberately. Sound check isn't just about loudness—it's about frequency balance and clarity. A good sound check might reveal that lower volume with better EQ serves the room better than louder and muddier.
  • Watch the room more than you listen to your own playing. Their reaction is the feedback that matters.
  • Take breaks if it feels right. You're not a jukebox.
  • Know when to simplify. The restaurant doesn't need your most complex arrangement. The most beautiful solo arrangement of your song might be too much for the room.
  • Build a relationship with each venue. After a couple gigs, you'll understand their crowd and their preferences.
  • Remember: You're there to enhance their experience, not showcase your abilities. The best compliment is when someone says, "The music was perfect for dinner," not, "That was an impressive solo."
