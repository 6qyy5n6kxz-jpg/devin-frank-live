Building a Practical Solo Musician Rig Without Carrying Too Much Gear

How to balance sound quality, functionality, and portability when you're playing restaurants, weddings, churches, and private events. Real-world constraints and actual setup choices.

By Devin Frank

Working musician, live performance gear experimenter

The Challenge: Doing It All Alone

When you're a solo performer, you're responsible for everything: your instruments, your sound reinforcement, your mixing, your setup, and your breakdown. Every pound of gear you bring is a pound you'll carry, often multiple times a night. Every cable is a potential tangled mess during setup. Every piece of equipment is one more thing that could fail mid-performance.

Yet you also need to sound professional. You need volume that reaches the whole room. You need clarity. You need to be able to adapt your setup to wildly different venues—a quiet restaurant dining room, a church sanctuary, a winery patio, a private home.

First Principle: Start With Your Sound Source

Before you buy any amplifiers, speakers, or mixing equipment, decide on your primary instrument. For me, that's the Studiologic Numa X Piano 73.

Why a keyboard as the centerpiece? Because it can do multiple things:

  • Piano: The foundation of my repertoire
  • Organ: Essential for churches and certain songs
  • Strings, pads, synths: Color and texture
  • Backup vocals processing: If I need to add effects

This one instrument can replace a traditional keyboard, an organ, and some backing track capabilities. That's efficiency.

Choosing a Mixing Approach

You have several mixing strategies as a solo performer:

Option 1: Powered Speakers (Simplest)

Plug directly into a powered speaker (like an EV Evolve 50M). One cable from your keyboard to the speaker, and you're done.

Pros: Minimal gear, fast setup, lightweight.

Cons: Limited flexibility. No room mixer means you can't easily add a second instrument, manage separate volumes, or add effects per channel.

Option 2: Small Mixer + Powered Speakers (Recommended for Most Gigs)

A compact mixer (like an Allen & Heath CQ-20B) gives you channels to mix multiple sources. With two powered speakers, you get:

  • Main outputs to both speakers
  • Channel volume control
  • Basic EQ per channel if needed
  • Room to add a second instrument or a DI input for acoustic guitar
  • Headphone output for a confidence monitor

Pros: Professional flexibility without being overkill. Works for restaurants, weddings, churches.

Cons: More cables, more setup time, more weight than powered speakers alone.

Speaker Configuration

For most solo gigs, I use two reasonably sized powered speakers: one main and one backup/room fill. The combination gives you:

  • Better coverage in a larger room
  • A backup in case one fails
  • Stereo capability for richer sound

I typically use EV Evolve 50M speakers—they're loud enough for most restaurants and churches, they're portable, and they're reliable.

Beyond the Keyboard and Speakers: Supporting Gear

A practical solo rig needs a few supporting pieces:

Acoustic Guitar DI

If you play acoustic guitar, you need a DI (direct injection) box like an LR Baggs Venue DI. It takes the output from a microphone or piezo pickup and sends it to your mixer. Adds weight: not much. Adds capability: enormous.

Backing Tracks

Some venues appreciate the depth of a full band on certain songs. Options include:

  • BeatBuddy: Drummer machine designed for musicians
  • OnSong: iPad app that plays backing tracks and manages setlists
  • Jamzone: Another backing track app I'm currently evaluating

I mix and match depending on the gig. Not every restaurant needs a full backing track. A lot of them prefer just you, your keyboard, and your guitar.

The Actual Setup I Use

For a typical restaurant or winery gig, I bring:

  • Studiologic Numa X Piano 73 (keyboard)
  • Allen & Heath CQ-20B (small mixer)
  • Two EV Evolve 50M powered speakers
  • Taylor GS Mini-e Koa Plus (acoustic guitar with built-in pickup)
  • LR Baggs Venue DI (for the guitar)
  • BeatBuddy or OnSong (for backing tracks, sometimes)
  • Various cables, a power strip, and a stand for the keyboard

Total weight is manageable for one person, though a vehicle is essential. Total setup time is usually 20–30 minutes for a full setup, including sound check.

What I Don't Carry (But Could)

The temptation is always there to add more:

  • A second keyboard (limits portability)
  • A full PA system with a separate power amp (overkill for most venues)
  • Multiple effect pedals (I prefer the effects built into the keyboard)
  • Extensive outboard gear (it adds weight, complexity, and single points of failure)

I revisit this list constantly. The key is asking: "Would this actually make me sound better for my typical gigs?" If the answer is no, it stays home.

The Evolution Continues

I'm currently experimenting with some additions:

  • The Neural DSP Quad Cortex, which could add more sophisticated effects and amp modeling
  • The MIDI Captain foot controller for more dynamic control during performance
  • Jamzone as a more versatile backing-track and setlist-management tool

But the core principle remains: only bring what serves the room and what you can actually manage by yourself.

Key Takeaways

Building a practical solo rig means:

  • Choose your primary instrument carefully. It should do multiple things if possible.
  • Prioritize flexibility over simplicity, but only where it matters. A small mixer is worth the extra weight; unnecessary outboard gear is not.
  • Speaker choice is everything. Invest in reliable, portable powered speakers that can fill different room sizes.
  • Support instruments (guitar, backing tracks) extend your capability without ballooning your rig.
  • Know what you're actually using. Gear you carry but never use is just dead weight.
  • Revisit it constantly. Your needs will change as you play different venues and build your repertoire.
