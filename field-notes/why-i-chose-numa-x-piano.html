Why I Chose the Studiologic Numa X Piano 73 for Live Performances

After years of experimenting with different keyboards, here's why the Numa X became my primary instrument for live gigs—and what it does well, plus what it doesn't.

By Devin Frank

Working musician, keyboard explorer, live performance gear tester

The Search for the Right Keyboard

Choosing a primary keyboard for live performance is a commitment. It's an instrument you'll carry to dozens of gigs, rely on in front of audiences, and fight with when something goes wrong. It needs to sound great, feel good under your fingers, be reliable, and not weigh a ton.

I spent years evaluating options. Some were too heavy. Some had beautiful piano sounds but weak organs. Some were fantastic stage instruments but terrible for the kinds of venues I was playing—restaurants where you need versatility more than cutting-edge synthesis.

The Studiologic Numa X Piano 73 was the first keyboard that felt like it checked all the boxes.

What I Needed From a Keyboard

My typical solo gig requires:

  • Great piano sounds – That's the foundation. Most of my setlist relies on convincing piano, not synthesizers or processed sounds.
  • Good organ sounds – Essential for churches, some jazz standards, and creating depth in a few key songs.
  • Versatile sound palette – Strings, pads, electric pianos, maybe a synth for texture.
  • Portability – I'm playing restaurants and wineries, sometimes twice in one night. A 100+ pound stage keyboard doesn't work.
  • Connectivity – MIDI out, audio outs, USB for backing tracks.
  • Reliability – I can't afford for it to fail mid-performance.
  • Playability – The keyboard action needs to feel responsive and musical, not cheap.

Why the Numa X

The Numa X Piano 73 was built by Studiologic, a company that makes instruments, not toys. Here's what pushed me toward this choice:

Piano Sound Quality

The piano engine is the real deal. Studiologic partnered with Fatar to create piano samples and modeling that sound convincing through a restaurant PA system. That was my primary test: does it sound like a real piano when I'm playing dinner music at a winery, or does it sound like cheap synthesis?

It passes that test. The piano sounds are warm, responsive to touch, and don't sound thin or processed through my powered speakers.

Organ Sounds

The organ engine includes proper drawbar emulation. It's not revolutionary, but it's solid. I can play a church gig and use the organ for certain songs without feeling like I'm faking it.

Weight and Portability

At about 35 pounds, the Numa X is heavier than a lightweight synth but light enough to carry by myself for most gigs. It's not a featherweight, but it's honest about what it is: a serious keyboard, not a toy.

73 Keys

73 keys is the perfect middle ground. It's enough range for most songs without being a full 88-key stage piano. It saves weight and size. Most solo restaurant gigs don't require the full 88-key range.

Build Quality

The keyboard action is semi-weighted. It's not as weighted as a full stage piano, but it's not weighted at all like a cheap synth. The keys feel intentional, and the build feels sturdy.

Connectivity and Integration

It has MIDI in and out, USB, and audio outs. This means I can:

  • Send MIDI to effect processors or other gear
  • Receive MIDI from foot controllers
  • Connect to backing track systems
  • Integrate with my live rig without excessive adapters

What I Like About It in Practice

After real-world use in dozens of gigs:

The Piano Engine is Forgiving

Good piano samples forgive playing that isn't perfect. The Numa X's piano samples sound musical even when you're tired, the restaurant is loud, or you're playing a song you don't know perfectly. That's worth a lot in a working musician's life.

The Sound Palette is Practical

The built-in sounds cover about 90% of what I actually use. I don't need 10,000 synth presets. I need good pianos, good organs, some strings, some pads, and maybe an electric piano for certain songs. The Numa X has all that.

Reliability

In my experience, it starts up, it connects, it plays. No weird crashes or unexpected behavior mid-gig. That's table stakes for an instrument, but it's worth noting when you're comparing to cheaper options.

What I Don't Love About It

No keyboard is perfect. Here's what I wish were different:

Limited Real Estate for Custom Controls

If I want to add knobs, buttons, or expression pedals to control things dynamically during a performance, the Numa X doesn't have as much customization as a larger stage instrument. That said, I'm currently exploring MIDI foot controllers to work around this limitation.

The Synth Engine is Basic

If I were a synth-heavy player, I'd want more waveform selection, more LFO options, more filter controls. But I'm not, so it doesn't matter for my actual work. Just know that if you're looking for deep synthesis, the Numa X is not that keyboard.

Sound Design Takes Work

The sound palette is practical, but creating truly unique sounds is limited. For someone who wants to create signature sounds or heavily process tones, this is a limitation.

How I'm Actually Using It

In my live rig, the Numa X connects to:

  • An Allen & Heath CQ-20B mixer (for mixing with acoustic guitar and backing tracks)
  • A small Effects processor or amp simulator when I want more sonic control
  • OnSong or BeatBuddy for backing tracks when appropriate
  • Eventually, a MIDI foot controller for more dynamic control

For most restaurant and winery gigs, I route it directly through the mixer and let the Numa X's onboard sounds do the work. It sounds great and requires minimal setup.

Is It Right for You?

The Studiologic Numa X Piano 73 is a good fit if you:

  • Play mostly piano-based music
  • Need to transport an instrument between gigs
  • Want real piano and organ sounds without spending $5,000+
  • Don't require extensive synthesis or sound design
  • Value reliability and build quality
  • Want to integrate with MIDI gear and backing tracks

It's probably not the right fit if you:

  • Need a full 88-key weighted hammer action
  • Play synth-heavy music
  • Require extensive sound design and synthesis
  • Need a keyboard that's a complete music production station

Bottom Line

After testing various keyboards, the Numa X Piano 73 became my choice because it's honest: it does the things it claims to do, it's built well, and it supports a professional solo performer's actual needs. It's not the fanciest keyboard on the stage, and it doesn't try to be. It's the one that gets me through dinner service at a restaurant and sounds great doing it.

For working musicians, that's the real test.
