Every Show Should Feel Different

Why I refuse to play the same show twice.

By Devin Frank

Working musician sharing practical lessons from the stage

I don’t think I’ve ever played the same show twice.

When people ask what kind of music I play, I usually tell them, “A little bit of everything.”

Country.

Classic rock.

90s.

Pop.

Singer-songwriter.

Requests.

That answer is true—but it also misses the point.

The goal has never been to build the perfect setlist.

The goal has always been to build the right setlist for the room.

That’s a subtle difference, but I think it’s one of the biggest things that separates musicians people remember from musicians people simply hear.

The Audience Changes. The Show Should Too.

When I first started playing live, I treated every show the same way.

I’d put together a setlist, arrange the songs in an order that seemed to make sense, and then show up and play it.

Over time I realized something.

The audience wasn’t the same.

So why was my show?

A winery on a Friday evening isn’t the same as a neighborhood restaurant on a Wednesday.

A church service isn’t the same as a graduation party.

A packed patio full of regulars isn’t the same as a quiet cocktail hour.

If the room changes, the music should change too.

More Songs Means More Options

That’s one of the reasons I’m always learning new songs even when I already have enough material to fill an entire evening.

Not because I need more songs.

Because I need more options.

Every new song gives me another tool.

Sometimes the right song changes the energy of a room.

Sometimes it’s the song that finally gets people singing along.

Sometimes it’s simply the song one person had hoped to hear all night.

The more tools I have available, the easier it is to shape the evening instead of forcing every audience into the same experience.

Give People a Reason to Come Back

I also don’t believe audiences should know exactly what they’re going to hear before they walk through the door.

If someone comes to see me in April and comes back again in August, I hope they leave feeling like they experienced a different show.

Some songs stay.

Others disappear for months.

Some get rearranged.

Some move from piano to guitar.

Some become duets.

Some never make it back into the rotation.

That unpredictability keeps performing fun for me—and hopefully keeps coming back fun for the audience.

The Setlist Is Important. The Audience Matters More.

This idea extends beyond the music itself.

Sometimes I’ll take requests.

Sometimes I’ll tell a story behind a song.

Sometimes I’ll slow the pace down because the room needs it.

Other times I’ll abandon my original plan entirely because the audience is ready to have fun.

The audience usually tells you what they want—you just have to pay attention.

The setlist is important.

But the audience matters more.

The Compliment I Want to Hear

One of the biggest compliments I can receive isn’t, “You sounded great.”

It’s hearing someone say:

“We’ll have to come back next time.”

That tells me they don’t expect a replay.

They expect another experience.

And that’s exactly what I’m trying to create.

Why I’m Writing Field Notes

I don’t claim to have all the answers.

Every show teaches me something.

Sometimes the lesson is about sound.

Sometimes it’s about song choice.

Sometimes it’s about reading people.

Those are the lessons I plan to share here.

If something works, I’ll tell you.

If something doesn’t, I’ll tell you that too.

Because I think musicians learn just as much from what went wrong as what went right.

Field Note

The best setlist isn’t the one that’s perfectly planned. It’s the one that’s willing to change.
